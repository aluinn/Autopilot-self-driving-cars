How it works

  1. Sense Each car fires five invisible sensor beams, like a simple lidar, and measures how close the nearest wall is in each direction.
  2. Think Those five readings, plus the car's speed, go into a tiny neural network: 70 adjustable numbers that decide whether to accelerate, brake, or steer left or right.
  3. Score At first every brain is random, so nearly every car crashes. Each car is scored on how many checkpoints it passes before it crashes, gets stuck, or turns round.
  4. Evolve The best brains become parents. They are copied, mixed together and randomly nudged to make the next generation. Nobody writes driving rules; trial, error and selection do the work.

In the real world Companies building self-driving cars and robots do the same in spirit: they train and test in simulation first, using tools like NVIDIA's simulators, so the software can fail millions of times safely before it ever meets a real road. Real systems use far larger neural networks fed by cameras, radar and lidar, and mostly learn from data rather than evolution, but the idea of learning by driving in a virtual world is the same.
